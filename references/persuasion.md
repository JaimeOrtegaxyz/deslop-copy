# Persuasion Reference — make it explain and sell

The other half of de-slopping: copy that doesn't just avoid sounding AI, but
actually makes the reader *get it* and *want it*. Positioning, hierarchy, hooks,
value props, proof, clarity.

## Principles (the rules to apply)

1. **Lead with the reader's problem or desired outcome, not your product's
   name.** Open on the world they already live in ("Spreadsheets break when
   three people edit them") so they recognize themselves before you introduce
   the fix.
2. **Make the concept legible in one sentence a smart outsider could repeat.**
   The hero must answer "what is this and who is it for" with zero jargon. If a
   friend couldn't paraphrase it after one read, it's too vague.
3. **Pick the competitive alternative and beat it explicitly.** Positioning is
   defined against what the reader would do otherwise (a rival, a spreadsheet,
   nothing). Name that frame so your unique value is obvious.
4. **Say ONE thing in the hero.** Don't cram three benefits into the headline;
   choose the single most important promise. The subhead and body carry the rest.
5. **Name the category so value is self-evident.** "Financial infrastructure for
   the internet" tells you scope and ambition instantly. The right frame ("the X
   for Y") does half the persuasion before any feature.
6. **Convert every feature into a "so what?" outcome.** After each feature, ask
   "so what does that get me?" and write THAT — "syncs offline" → "keep working
   on the plane."
7. **Prove claims with concrete nouns and numbers, not adjectives.** Replace
   "powerful, seamless, robust" with "deploys in 90 seconds," "trusted by 10,000+
   teams." Specificity is what makes a claim believable.
8. **Write to "you," cut the "we."** Rewrite lines that start with the company
   name or "We built…" to start with "you" or a verb. The reader cares about
   their outcome, not your effort.
9. **Cut to the verb.** Strong active verbs over noun-phrases: "Ship faster"
   beats "Enables the acceleration of your shipping process."
10. **Be specific enough to sound confident, not hyped.** "The fastest email
    experience ever made" reads as confidence; "the world's most amazing
    platform" reads as insecurity.
11. **Use tension, not clickbait, in the hook.** Name a real friction the reader
    feels ("Your CI is the bottleneck"); don't withhold the point to tease.
12. **Earn abstraction with proof underneath.** A lofty hero line ("Meet your
    thinking partner") is only allowed if the next layer shows concrete
    capability ("Break down problems together," named use cases).
13. **Match the message to reader awareness.** If they feel the pain, name it and
    solve fast (PAS); if they don't know the category exists, teach the problem
    first (AIDA).
14. **Front-load specific social proof.** "Trusted by teams that ship" + named
    logos beats "trusted by thousands." A specific customer + result out-persuades
    a generic badge.
15. **Read it aloud and vary the rhythm.** Mix short punches with one longer
    line. A flat paragraph kills momentum; a tricolon on the *spine* (not every
    line) creates it.
16. **Keep reading level low and sentences short.** Plain words a busy reader
    skims in one pass. Big words sound pretentious and lose people.
17. **Show, don't tell, the transformation.** Instead of "easy," demonstrate
    ("start sending emails in minutes"). Let them picture the after-state.
18. **End every section with a reason to act, not just a description.** Tie the
    close to the reader's payoff and one clear next step.

## Before / After patterns

- **Product-first → Reader-first**
  Before: "Acme is a cloud-native observability platform."
  After: "Find out why your app is slow — before your users do."
- **Feature → Outcome**
  Before: "Real-time collaborative editing with conflict resolution."
  After: "Three people, one doc, zero 'final_FINAL_v2.'"
- **Adjective soup → Concrete proof**
  Before: "A powerful, seamless, enterprise-grade deployment solution."
  After: "Push to git. Live in 90 seconds. Every branch gets its own URL."
- **Vague benefit → Specific transformation**
  Before: "Boost your team's productivity and streamline workflows."
  After: "Cut your standup from 30 minutes to a glance."
- **We-focused → You-focused**
  Before: "We've built the most advanced AI engine on the market."
  After: "Ask in plain English. Get an answer you can ship."
- **Hype headline → Confident specific**
  Before: "The #1 revolutionary game-changing email tool!"
  After: "Email for developers. The best way to reach humans instead of spam folders."
- **Hedged → Direct**
  Before: "We aim to help teams potentially reduce some onboarding friction."
  After: "New hires ship code on day one."
- **Everything-at-once → One thing**
  Before: "Invoicing, payroll, expenses, reporting, forecasting, and tax — all in one."
  After: "Get paid faster." (rest demoted to a feature row)
- **Abstract category → Legible frame**
  Before: "A next-generation knowledge synchronization layer."
  After: "The wiki your team actually keeps up to date."
- **Tease (clickbait) → Tension (honest hook)**
  Before: "You won't believe what this tool does to your bug count…"
  After: "Most bugs ship because nobody saw the diff. Now everyone does."

## Frameworks worth using (and their limits)

- **PAS (Problem → Agitate → Solution).** For a reader who already feels the
  pain. *Limit:* agitation turns manipulative if the pain is invented — skip the
  agitate beat when the problem is obvious.
- **AIDA (Attention → Interest → Desire → Action).** For new/unaware audiences or
  longer pages. *Limit:* bloats into a formula-funnel when the reader already
  knows what they want.
- **The "so what?" test.** Converts any feature to a benefit. *Limit:*
  over-applied, it strips the concrete specifics that make copy credible — stop
  when the next "so what" would erase the proof.
- **April Dunford positioning (alternative → unique attributes → value → who
  cares → category).** Decides *what to say first.* *Limit:* a strategy tool, not
  voice — tells you the message, not the words; can read like a B2B template if
  you stop there.
- **The "one thing" rule.** Forces hierarchy so the hero isn't a list. *Limit:*
  oversimplifies genuinely multi-product platforms — use a clear primary line +
  a structured feature layer.
- **FAB (Feature → Advantage → Benefit) / Wiebe's 7 Sweeps.** Systematically
  upgrades feature copy for believability. *Limit:* mechanical application yields
  identical three-clause sentences — vary the structure.
- **Tricolon / rule of three.** Memorable rhythm, implied completeness. *Limit:*
  a tic when every section is three staccato words; reserve it for the spine.

## Exemplars (real copy)

- **Stripe** — "Financial infrastructure to grow your revenue." — Defines a whole
  category; frames Stripe as bedrock, not a checkout button. The scope *is* the pitch.
- **Linear** — "The product development system for teams and agents." /
  "Purpose-built for planning and building products." — Names a *system*, claims
  a category, earns the abstraction with concrete intent.
- **Vercel** — "Develop. Preview. Ship." / "The fastest workflow for frontend
  teams." — A three-verb tricolon that *is* the workflow; memorable and repeatable.
- **Resend** — "Email for developers" / "The best way to reach humans instead of
  spam folders." — Names the exact reader; reframes deliverability as a vivid
  human outcome.
- **Superhuman** — "The fastest email experience ever made." — A single concrete
  superlative (speed); confident, not hypey.
- **Claude (Anthropic)** — "Meet your thinking partner" / "The AI for problem
  solvers." — Sells a relationship and transformation, then grounds it with a
  defined audience and concrete use cases.
- **Basecamp** — "The refreshingly straightforward project management system
  that's rock-solid and easy to use." — Human, conversational voice that names
  the reader's real frustration (bloated PM tools) and answers it plainly.
- **Retool** — "Apps that mean business" — A pun that doubles as positioning:
  internal/business apps *and* serious, production-grade. Three words.
- **Notion** — "The AI workspace that works for you." / "Meet the night shift." —
  Reframes AI as labor done *for* you; "night shift" is a concrete, ownable
  metaphor for an abstract feature.
- **Figma** — "One workspace for your entire product development process." — The
  subhead does the legibility work by stating concretely what it replaces.

## What to AVOID (persuasion failure modes)

- **Hype inflation.** Stacked superlatives signal weak proof; every unverifiable
  adjective lowers trust. Replace with one concrete number or noun.
- **Feature soup.** Capabilities with no "so what" make the reader translate to
  benefit — most won't. Lead with outcome; demote the list.
- **Vague benefits.** "Boost productivity," "unlock growth" are interchangeable
  across a thousand products. Name the specific before/after.
- **We-speak.** Copy about the company's effort and architecture instead of the
  reader's payoff. The reader is the protagonist; the product is the tool.
- **Hedging.** "Aim to," "help you potentially" leak doubt. State the outcome or
  cut the line.
- **Trying to say everything.** A hero that pitches every audience pitches no
  one. Choose, sequence, demote.
- **Abstraction with no floor.** A lofty tagline with nothing concrete beneath it
  is a logo without a product. Every abstraction needs proof one layer down.
- **Borrowed jargon / category fog.** Chained buzzwords hide the concept. If a
  smart outsider can't picture it, rewrite in plain nouns.
- **Clickbait tone.** Withholding the point insults a professional reader and
  erodes the credibility you need to convert. Name the stakes, don't tease them.
